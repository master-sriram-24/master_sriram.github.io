A NOTE FROM BEHIND THE CAMERA

It started with
looking up.

I didn't begin with a dedicated camera or dark-sky destinations. I began on a terrace, with a Google Pixel 4a and a curiosity about the stars above my hometown.

CHAPTER 01

APRIL 2022

It began on a terrace.

I used to sleep on the terrace and watch the stars. In April 2022, I got my first phone, a Google Pixel 4a. Its astrophotography mode could build a long exposure from multiple shorter captures, and the results felt magical compared with the night photographs I had seen from other phones.

I started photographing the night sky in my hometown. I didn't know where this curiosity would lead, but I knew I wanted to keep looking.

CHAPTER 02

APRIL–MAY 2023

Chasing the Milky Way in a city of lights.

After moving to Chennai for higher studies, I watched a technology video about capturing the Milky Way with a phone. I was curious enough to save up for a tripod and try it myself.

On April 22, I captured 12 photographs and stacked them in Sequator. I could just make out the colour of the Milky Way's core. Two days later, I tried again with 138 frames. Instead of the Milky Way image I expected, I discovered star trails, an early-morning blue sky, and golden clouds.

On May 24 and 26, I tried again with 418 and 50 frames. Chennai's light pollution made the results difficult to recognise without an explanation—but I kept experimenting.

CHAPTER 03

JUNE–NOVEMBER 2023

The night I finally saw it.

During a holiday back home, I set up my Pixel 4a and tripod in the backyard and stayed beside it through the night. When I checked the first image, the Milky Way was finally there—clear enough for me to tell my family that I'd captured it properly.

The next morning, I stacked the files and was amazed by what I had made. Later that year, on November 24, I experimented with light painting and wrote my name beneath the stars.

CHAPTER 04

2024

A comet, a coastline, and a shared sky.

In February, I photographed the Milky Way from my light-polluted hometown with my Pixel 4a. I returned on April 19 from a different angle, and on April 20 I captured a timelapse of stars and moving clouds. In June, I continued experimenting with night skies and clouds; in Chennai, I framed stars among trees and buildings.

Then came October 1, when comet C/2023 A3 (Tsuchinshan–ATLAS) appeared in my frame at the beach. The comet sat above the Bay of Bengal, with stars and clouds around it, colourful pre-sunrise sky above, and the shadows of my friends and me on the sand below.

I returned on October 2 with more friends so they could witness the event too. Seeing them enjoy it made the experience as memorable as the photograph.

CHAPTER 05

MAY 3–4, 2025 · KODAIKANAL

Thirteen minutes under a darker sky.

My first solo astrophotography trip—and my first time using a dedicated camera for the night sky—took me to a remote village near Kodaikanal, away from city lights. I had a Sony α6700, its kit lens, and a Sigma 35mm lens.

I photographed the Milky Way beside a tree. When I stacked the frames during post-processing, I was amazed by the detail that emerged. This became one of the best Milky Way photographs I had processed. The capture took 13 minutes, but processing it took more than 24 hours of learning, trial and error, and reprocessing.

Looking into the Sagittarius region, I could identify the Lagoon Nebula (M8), Trifid Nebula (M20), and Eagle Nebula (M16). I also made my first Milky Way panorama. The trip, organised by Vijay Basker and Abinesh, brought new friends from different backgrounds into my life.

CHAPTER 06

JUNE 22–23, 2025 · VATTAVADA

Learning to leave room for the weather.

June 22 was full of rain. Was I disappointed? Yes. But I had travelled solo, and I started talking to the strangers who had arrived as a group. By the end, they had become friends—people from different fields, many of them not photographers at all.

The next night was rainy too. After 2 a.m., the rain finally stopped and the clouds began to move. We managed to capture the Milky Way through the gaps in the clouds.

This trip taught me a lesson that no photograph could: plan carefully, but stay flexible. The weather, the people, and the unexpected are all part of the journey.

CHAPTER 07

DECEMBER 14–15, 2025 · POOMBARAI

A night full of meteors.

I travelled to Poombarai, Kodaikanal, for the Geminids meteor shower. On the first night, I stayed awake through the whole night, watching for meteors and trying to capture them.

On the second night, I photographed a 180-degree panorama of the winter Milky Way, along with M31, the Andromeda Galaxy, the Pleiades—also known as the Seven Sisters—and the Orion Nebula. I also brought a film camera on an astrophotography trip for the first time and made some star-trail photographs.

Spending the whole night talking and having fun with people who had been strangers at the start made the trip special. I came away with more than photographs: I learnt how to manage time on a trip, how to pack thoughtfully, and what to bring—and what to leave behind.

ABOUT THE PHOTOGRAPHER

I'm Sriram, an astrophotographer and visual artist exploring light, colour, movement, and our relationship with the world around us.
